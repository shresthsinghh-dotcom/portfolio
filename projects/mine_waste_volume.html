Estimating Mine-Waste Volumes from Elevation Data

The project set out to tune a terrain-based volume estimator toward USGS. What it produced is more honest and more useful than tuning: a fully characterized method with a stated domain of validity, and a rigorous account of exactly where and why it fails.

Overview

Volume is the term that is missing at scale.

There are hundreds of thousands of mines across the continental United States: tungsten, gold, coal, and more. The central hypothesis of this critical-materials hub is that each of these mines has waste dumps, and that these dumps may contain both highly abundant and accessible amounts of critical materials. But although locations and rough polygons for thousands of mine features — chief among which are the waste dumps — are available, the volumes of many of these sites are genuinely a mystery.

The value of a dump is, roughly, Value = Volume × Grade × Price × Recovery — cubic metres of waste, times critical-mineral concentration, times commodity price, times the fraction economically extractable. Grade comes from assays; price from markets; recovery from metallurgy. Volume is the term that is missing at scale, because while the inventory gives footprints, it rarely gives depths. Everything that follows is an attempt to supply that one number — volume — from remote elevation data alone, so that a prospector holding a grade estimate can convert a map polygon into a defensible tonnage.

This project delivers one term in that valuation. It is the companion to my Tungsten Queen geochemistry study — that work looks closely at a single site's grade; this one supplies the volume across a hundred sites in four states.

The Method

To find the volume, we need two things: the exterior shape of the feature, and the ground beneath it.

Without previously drawn maps of the mine before mining began, it is impossible to know what the ground looks like using imaging alone. Therefore it must be extrapolated using the surrounding exposed surface — and subtracting the exterior from that extrapolated ground provides the volume of the site.

1. Footprint & DEM

DEMs are three-dimensional maps of the ground surface, derived from airborne lidar and photogrammetry rather than from satellite photographs. We pull the dump footprint plus a surrounding buffer and download the DEM.

2. Reconstruct the base

A thin-plate spline fits a perfectly smooth surface across scattered points — the shape a flexible, infinitely thin sheet of metal would take, pinned to specific heights and left to bend with minimal resistance. Anchored to the natural ground ringing the dump, it rebuilds the buried base.

3. Integrate the volume

Subtract the extrapolated base from the current exterior surface and integrate the positive difference across the footprint — the material the dump added.

4. Validate

A series of validation squares — one steep, one normal, one flat patch of natural ground — reveal that the algorithm is adequately successful at recreating ground from nearby data.

Visual Results

Every site is processed into a diagnostic that shows the reconstruction, the validation, and the volume — making both the successes and the failures legible at a glance.

Reconstructing the buried surface

A render of the Hallman-Beam site as it was processed. The green lines indicate the buffer for the extrapolation, the squares revealing the validation, the mask in orange. The DEM data is overlayed over the extrapolated ground, and the cross-section shows the current surface over the reconstructed base — the shaded gap the dump fill.

Does it recreate real ground?

Held-out validation squares give bias −0.11 m (flat), −0.19 m (normal), −0.20 m (steep). The spline reproduces undisturbed ground to within ~0.2 m even on the steepest natural patch available. The reconstruction is accurate, not suspect — loud colors would mean loud errors, and steep extrapolations do struggle only a bit.

Regional scale: correcting the bias

Raw, the method recovers 42% of the reference — the raw pipeline under-counts total regional waste by roughly half (−58%). The log-ratio correction lands the corrected total within −4 to −6% of the USGS sum: a usable answer to how much waste is in a region.

When it works: flat & plateau dumps

Twin Buttes, one of the more successful entries — estimate 2,619,397 m³, within +1.0% of USGS, in-band. A dump sitting on a flat plain has low perimeter relief: walk around its base and the ground stays at roughly the same height, so the surrounding ground is a reliable guide to what lies buried.

Ruling out resolution

A hundred-fold increase in resolution — 10 m to 1 m — changed the reconstructed base and the volume negligibly (−14.5% to −14.6% here). Resolution is not the bottleneck; the spline is the same, and the buried deficit lies beneath it.

Knowing what it can measure

Perimeter relief is a rough proxy for how trustworthy the buried-ground estimate is. In-method plateau dumps cluster near zero; flagged valley and hillslope fills, where the buried surface is more likely to plunge in ways the edges cannot reveal, fall far below — reported as brackets, not measurements.

What It Establishes

A bounded result, not an unfinished one.

In short, the work is not unfinished so much as bounded: it has done what surface data permits, characterized precisely where that runs out, and built the mechanism to continue the moment the missing data becomes available.

A characterized limit

The method works for flat-lying and plateau dumps, where the perimeter approximates pre-mining ground and the residual is a modest base-definition offset. It fails, structurally, on valley and hillslope fills, where the buried valley floor plunges below the perimeter the spline is anchored to — a void surface terrain cannot see. That failure is not a bug to be fixed with a better spline, a finer DEM, a drainage heuristic, or more training sites; each was tried and each hit the same wall.

Usable at regional scale

Surface observables can proxy the buried deficit well enough to halve the aggregate bias, but cannot recover per-site volume, because the missing information is not on the surface. At the basin scale, the log-ratio correction reduces aggregate bias from −58% to within −4 to −6% of the USGS total — a usable answer to how much waste is in a region.

Built to cross the gap

On a synthetic valley fill with known ground truth, folding real interior control moved the estimate from −13% to +11% — the mechanism reshapes the base as intended. The control-point hook stands ready to cross the floor the moment field data exists, matching USGS where truth is available and falling back to a characterized estimate everywhere else.

The Research Arc

The dead ends are load-bearing.

This is a research record: each dead end rules out a hypothesis and sharpens why the problem is hard. The full write-up and diagnostic poster are linked below.

Site selection & DEM sourcing

The first step is to find the best possible sites to build an algorithm around: sites that are very large, with easily distinguishable footprints, and a flat surrounding terrain. Arizona and North Carolina offer significantly less terrain challenge than the mountainous regions of Virginia and Montana, and mine dumps are easier than tailings — tailings ponds specifically being a negative elevation relative to their surroundings. For these reasons the focus was kept to mine dumps in North Carolina and Arizona.

Base reconstruction — the core method

The disturbance mask would not be used to extrapolate; it is not natural ground. Instead the bounding box is enlarged and a buffer created around the mask to provide adequate data. The reconstruction is accurate — yet Hallman-Beam still came in ~14% below USGS, and that gap proved robust: going from a 300 m ring to a 600 m ring plus a 300 m topo collar moved the estimate about 0.3 points. The gap is robust to how much surrounding terrain the spline sees.

The drainage hypothesis — an instructive dead end

It was a reasonable idea, and on idealized test cases it worked. But on real sites it made the estimates worse, for a fundamental reason: the modern stream, seen in the elevation data, has been diverted around the dump, so it no longer marks where the buried channel actually ran. The conclusion was not "drainage doesn't matter" but "the buried channel cannot be recovered from the surface alone." So the hypothesis was documented as an instructive dead end rather than a fix — it sharpened why the problem is hard, which is itself a result.

Empirical correction & the error floor

A log-ratio model — ln(USGS/estimate) on perimeter relief and the "blue" clip fraction — reached R² = 0.86 and a median near 34%. Perimeter relief alone carried no predictive signal (R² = 0.00); only combined with the blue-clip fraction did a usable model emerge. Scaling from 20 to 100 sites, adding predictors moved the leave-one-out error negligibly while in-sample R² crept up — the textbook signature of a model that has reached its ceiling.

Ruling out the usual suspects

A hundred-fold increase in resolution changed the volume negligibly; resolution is not the bottleneck. And refitting the identical control points with five kernels, the four stable kernels agree with thin-plate at the median to the rounding — a 0.0% typical shift. Thin-plate is the consensus surface, not an arbitrary one; no kernel improves on it, because the residual is buried-surface and invisible to any interpolator.

The control-point hook — field-data-ready

USGS's own volumes are not derived from surrounding terrain but from field work — pre-mining topographic maps and drill cores that fix the true depth to contact. A modern DEM cannot manufacture any of that; what the pipeline can do is accept it when it exists. On a synthetic valley fill, folding real interior control moved the estimate from −13% to +11%. No real data was available during the project, so this stands as validated infrastructure — and the single result that would most advance this work is not more code, but one real dataset.
