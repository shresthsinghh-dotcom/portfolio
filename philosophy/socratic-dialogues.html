Socratic Dialogues

Moral Inquiry & the Dialectic Method

The Socratic Period

Socrates believed that writing was a futile endeavour insofar as it could not engage with its reader. His method of inquity, elenchus, revolved around dynamically questioning assumptions and beliefs. Thus, his teachings were never written down by him, and we would likely have lost most of them, were it not for his student, Plato. These dialogues are the most vivid illustrations of his philosophical approach, recorded through the lens of his pupil. We begin with the apology.

The Apology

One of the most important records of Socratic dialogue, this text is Socrates' defense at his trial for crimes of impiety. I consider this to be one of the earliest and most prominent examples of anti-establishment intellectual resistance — a man standing before the state, armed only with reason, and refusing to compromise his principles even when faced with death.

One of the most important revelations that Socrates reached was mentioned here. The Oracle of Delphi, considered the voice of the gods, stated that Socrates was the wisest of all men. Socrates was confused by this proclamation, believing himself to be ignorant. He thus set out to disprove the Oracle by questioning those reputed to be wise — politicians, poets, artisans — only to discover that their wisdom was superficial, built on assumption rather than genuine understanding.

The Oracle's pronouncement was not that Socrates possessed knowledge, but that he alone recognized the limits of his own understanding.

Socrates employed scathing arguments, questioning his prosecutor Meletus and silencing his claims with ease. He argued firmly that he was not only not guilty, but rather necessary for society, comparing himself to a gadfly that stings a great and sluggish horse — Athens itself — into moral wakefulness.

Despite the strength of his arguments, I believe Socrates knew this trial would be his last. To me, this only serves to illustrate brilliantly just how grounded the philosopher was in his beliefs. He did not argue that his life was worth saving; rather, he argued that it was being taken unfairly. He pleaded not for life, but for rational thought.

Socrates was ultimately sentenced to death by a narrow vote of 280 to 250. He joked that had a few men changed their minds, perhaps he would have lived. Greek law at the time allowed the defendant to propose a counter-penalty — Socrates suggested free meals at the public expense, the honor given to Olympic victors, before reluctantly proposing a small fine.

Socrates administered a final warning to his condemners. He argued that at seventy, he really had not much longer to live anyway. The real tragedy, the real symbol of this trial was not the death of a man, but the willingness of a society to silence the one voice asking it to think. His last words to the jury were not of bitterness, but of philosophical certainty: that no evil can come to a good man, either in life or in death.

Euthyphro

Euthyphro is the name of the man who conversed with Socrates as he awaited trial for the aforementioned crime of impiety. This text is an excellent example of the Socratic method in action, with relentless questioning and a dialectic manner that ends without an answer — allowing the reader to debate and answer their own questions.

Euthyphro represents the Sophists: unable to defend a stance they so stubbornly hold. His arguments are quickly disassembled by Socrates, who used humility as lure to break them down.

Is an act pious because it is loved by the gods, or is it loved by the gods because it is pious?

This foundational question forces us to confront the nature of moral authority itself. If an action is holy because it is loved by divine power, then said divine power becomes the arbiter of justice with no justification or morality behind its actions but its own will. Thus, it is unchecked authority — power without principle.

But if the gods love an action because it is pious, then piety exists independently of the gods, and they are merely recognizing a truth that transcends even them. This implies a moral framework that stands above divine authority.

The beauty of Socrates' conversation here is laid out in his search for an answer, and that he doesn't provide one. Socrates wasn't wise because he knew all, but because all he knew was that he didn't know anything. He makes Euthyphro realize, through his own words, that his understanding of piety is circular and ungrounded.

Socrates states that impiety is what is hated by the gods, and piety is what is loved by them, to Euthyphro's affirmation. Socrates thus establishes that every man does what he deems noble. As the gods would often fight, impiety would have to be only that which they all hate, and piety that which they all love. All that is just is not necessarily pious, but all that is pious must be just.

Thus the dialogue reveals a deeper truth: that moral reasoning cannot rest on authority alone, whether divine or human. It must be grounded in something that can withstand the scrutiny of honest questioning — and that the willingness to question is itself the beginning of wisdom.

The Sophists

The Sophists were a class of itinerant intellectuals who, unlike the Presocratics, were less concerned with the nature of the cosmos and more with teaching rhetoric, argumentation, and practical wisdom — for a fee. They represent a pivotal counterpoint to the Socratic tradition, and understanding them is essential to understanding Socrates himself.
