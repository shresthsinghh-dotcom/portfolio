Engineering is where I build things; writing is where I work out what I think about them. These two essays are the longest arguments I have made in print. One asks what technology is doing to the people who use it. The other traces how a generation of Russian sons came to reject everything their fathers believed, and what that rejection cost.

Essay · Technology & Society

The Great American Train

On technology, surveillance, and the faith that progress is going somewhere good.

We have built our whole social and professional structure on the premise that the quality of our lives can be equated to the improvement of our tools. This essay argues that the premise is a faith rather than a finding — and that the rapid rise of technology has functioned as a means of control, because it arrived as an instrument of surveillance.

Working from Neil Postman's Amusing Ourselves to Death and Shoshana Zuboff's account of surveillance capitalism, it argues that technology is ecological rather than optional: it reshapes the environment of everyone in it, including those who never opted in, while its advantages accrue to a narrow few.

Essay · History & Literature

Shadows in the Light

Fathers, sons, and the nihilist forefathers of revolution.

Russia emerged from the Napoleonic wars as the arbiter of Europe and then slid, over half a century, from prestige into stagnation. This essay follows that decline through the quarrel between Westernizers and Slavophiles, the humiliation of the Crimean War, and an emancipation that freed the serfs without freeing them from anything.

Reading Turgenev's Fathers and Sons against that history, it argues that nihilism was not a philosophy that arrived from outside but one that emerged dialectically between two generations — sons who could not escape their fathers, and fathers who could not answer their sons.
