Book II

Socrates & the Sophists

Socrates represents the most radical reorientation in the history of philosophy: a shift from cosmology to ethics, from asking what the world is to asking how one ought to live. He resolves nothing of the metaphysical dispute between Heraclitus and Parmenides — he sidesteps it, insisting that clarity about virtue, justice, and the good life is more urgent than speculation about nature.

The Apology

399 BCE · Athens

No evil can come to a good man, either in life or in death.

The Trial Elenchus Oracle of Delphi
⏱ 4 min read

One of the most important records of Socratic dialogue, this text is Socrates' defense on his trial for crimes of impiety. I consider this to be one of the earliest and most prominent examples of anti-intellectualism, where Socrates was charged with "corrupting the youth" for speaking plainly and dissecting the arguments of his sophist contemporaries.

One of the most important revelations that Socrates reached was mentioned here. The Oracle of Delphi, considered the voice of the gods, stated that Socrates was the wisest of all men. Socrates was confused, for how could this be the case? He knew only that he knew ultimately nothing. He thus came to the conclusion that if the acknowledgement of this deep humility made him the wisest, then all those who claimed to know a lot were lying, or misguided, but unwise nonetheless. This is a core idea of Socrates' teachings. Accept that you know nothing, and cast aside your presumptions on reality.

Socrates employed scathing arguments, questioning his prosecutor Meletus and silencing his claims with ease. He argued firmly that he was not only not guilty, but rather necessary for society, comparing his own methods, the enigmatic Elenchus, to being that of a fly to a horse. Its constant poking is what motivates the horse into action, rather than letting it slumber on. Thus, Athenian democracy was the horse, and Socrates, the ever-irritating motivation, the fly.

Despite the strength of his arguments, I believe Socrates knew this trial would be his last. To me, this only serves to illustrate brilliantly just how grounded the philosopher was in his beliefs. He stared into the face of death, and instead of begging for his life, or appealing to what his jury perhaps wished to hear, he only justified himself. He asserted that if he had the choice between changing his ways or dying a thousand more times, he would always choose the latter.

He did not argue that his life was worth saving; rather, he argued that it was being taken unfairly. He pleaded not for life, but rather for rational thought.

"...I trust you, and God himself, to decide about me in the way that shall be best for me and you."

Socrates was ultimately sentenced to death by a narrow vote of 280 to 250. He joked that had a few men changed their minds, perhaps he would have lived. Greek law at the time allowed the defendant to propose a counter punishment to the one the jury deemed fit, and Socrates took this opportunity to calmly stun the room. He asserted that for his service to Athens, not only should he not be sentenced to death, but that he should be provided with free room and a great meal at the hall. This counterclaim, a true display of principle, was rejected.

Socrates administers a final warning to his condemners. He argues that at 70, he really had not much longer to live anyway. The real tragedy, the real symbol of this trial was not the death of a man, but rather the forced silencing of critique upon society. He warned this would ultimately hurt the jurors and society more than him, but that he was not angry with this verdict. His last words to the jury were a request to ensure that his sons would be punished in the same way as him, if they ever strayed from the path he had laid. He then accepted his death calmly, stating:

"And now it is time to go, I to die, and you to live; but which one of us goes to a better thing is unknown to all but God."

Euthyphro

399 BCE · Outside the Athenian courts

Is an act pious because it is loved by the gods, or is it loved by the gods because it is pious?

The Piety Dilemma Dialectic Method
⏱ 3 min read

Euthyphro is the name of the man who conversed with Socrates, who was awaiting trial for the aforementioned crime of impiety. This text is an excellent example of the Socratic method in action, with relentless questioning and a dialectic manner that ends without an answer, allowing the reader to debate and answer their own questions.

Euthyphro represents the sophists: unable to defend a stance they so stubbornly hold. His arguments are quickly disassembled by Socrates, who used humility as lure to break them down.

This foundational text asks us to confront a very difficult question: Is an act pious because it is loved by the gods, or is it loved by the gods because it is pious? This dialogue between Socrates and Euthyphro is an interesting look into the revolutionary thought that Socrates created, and the stubborn, shallow Sophists that contrasted his thought.

Is an act pious because it is loved by the gods, or is it loved by the gods because it is pious?

If an action is holy, because it is loved by divine power, then said divine power becomes the arbiter of justice, with no justification or morality behind its actions but its own. Thus, it is unchecked and dictatorial. However, if an action is loved because it is holy, then the power that loves it is but a servant to a higher, more powerful standard of morality, begging the question: why follow a power subservient to another? This is the central question of Euthyphro.

The beauty of Socrates' conversation here is laid out in his search for an answer, and that he doesn't provide one. Socrates wasn't wise because he knew all, but because all he knew was that he didn't know anything. The conversation goes as follows:

Socrates states that impiety is what is hated by the gods, and piety is what loved by them, to Euthyphro's affirmation. Socrates thus states that every man does what he deems noble. Thus, the question is never if should injustice be punished, but rather if an injustice was even committed. Who committed the injustice?

Thus, as the gods would often fight, impiety would have to only be that what they all hate, and piety to be what they all love. All that is just is not pious, but all that is pious must be just. Thus piety is an extension of justice, but what part? If piety is merely that which attends to god, then does it improve him? Surely not, thus piety is that which pleases him. And thus, we return only to the central question. Is piety just that what god loves, or is it that what is holy?
