My Engineering

From concept to prototype — designing systems that balance performance, safety, and practicality.

Overview

My engineering work centers on mechanical system design, prototyping, and testing, with an emphasis on understanding how individual components come together into a reliable system. I am particularly interested in designs that balance performance, safety, and practicality under real-world constraints.

Through both course-based and team-based projects, I have worked through the full engineering design process, from problem definition and concept development to fabrication, testing, and refinement. My approach emphasizes clear decision-making, attention to tolerances, and learning from testing results to improve performance.

Team Project

Duke Aero

Nose Cone

Mold Design & Fabrication

As one of the rocket's most aerodynamically critical components, the nose cone required precise geometry and a high-quality surface finish. I led the design and fabrication effort, starting with a Von Kármán profile modeled in CAD to minimize transonic drag. The full-scale plug was 3D-printed in sections and assembled into a continuous mandrel, which served as the master geometry for mold creation.

I then laid fiberglass over the plug to produce a two-piece female mold with a smooth inner surface. The final nose cone was fabricated by laying carbon fiber into the female mold and curing under vacuum bag pressure, achieving a dimensional tolerance of less than 1 mm across the full part length. This process was developed from scratch to comply with updated competition regulations for the current season.

Nose Cone Simulation

Finite Element Analysis

Before constructing the nose cone, I had to ensure that its shape would actually hold up to the stresses of supersonic flight. Thus, I took the CAD file and uploaded it to ANSYS workbench, assigned its various components the right materials, and conducted a number of important finite element analyses for stress and deformation.

Note that the first image is the cone itself; the tip is 6061 arospace-grade aluminum with high strength-to-weight ratios. The rest of the cone is woven carbon fiber. The images show the equivalent stress, equivalent elastic strain, and total deformation under the applied load.

Components

Forged Structural Parts

The airbrake system demanded components that were both structurally rigid and lightweight enough to deploy reliably at high velocity. I designed and fabricated the airbrake paddles using a forged carbon fiber composite process: chopped carbon fiber was combined with epoxy resin and compression-molded in a two-part aluminum-reinforced mold under controlled pressure and temperature.

This approach produced near-net-shape parts with consistent fiber distribution and high strength-to-weight ratios. Each paddle was post-processed, inspected for dimensional accuracy, and fit-checked against the airbrake deployment assembly to verify clearances and actuation geometry.

Airframe

Carbon Fiber Tube Layup

The body tubes form the primary airframe structure and must withstand significant axial and bending loads during flight. I co-led the tube fabrication effort, which involved wrapping pre-cut carbon fiber sheets around a precision-machined cylindrical mandrel, applying a wet layup of epoxy resin, and vacuum-bagging the assembly to consolidate the laminate and remove excess resin.

After curing, the tubes were trimmed to final length and inspected for surface quality and wall thickness uniformity. These tubes directly comprise the flight-ready airframe sections used by Duke Aero in competition.

Project

Adaptive Ball Launcher

Electromechanical System Design

Project Team

This project was completed as part of EGR 101, a course focused on engineering design and technical communication. I worked closely with a small team to define requirements, divide responsibilities, and coordinate design decisions throughout the project.

Low-Fidelity Prototype

After discussing design goals and constraints, we built a low-fidelity prototype to quickly test initial concepts and gather early feedback. This trebuchet-style launcher allowed us to explore basic launch mechanics, but early testing showed that the design raised safety concerns and lacked sufficient control.

Medium-Fidelity Prototype

The medium-fidelity prototype introduced a more rigid frame and a spring-loaded mechanism, allowing us to better evaluate launch force and trajectory. Testing showed improved consistency and control, and this version served as the foundation for the final design.

Final Prototype

The final prototype integrates a modular frame with low-force activation and an Arduino-controlled linear drive. The system converts electrical input into controlled mechanical motion, allowing users to launch the ball with minimal force while achieving consistent and accurate performance.

CAD Design

Custom Plug Assembly

I designed the plug assembly in CAD as a three-part mechanism: the plug body, the gate linkage, and the shooter channel. The plug interfaces with a linear drive system via a threaded connection, which retracts the gates and shooter rearward against a preloaded spring. Upon release, the stored elastic energy drives the gates forward through the shooter channel, launching the ball with consistent force and trajectory.

The geometry was iterated to minimize friction in the gate travel path and ensure reliable disengagement at the trigger point. Tolerances on the threaded interface and guide rails were held tight to prevent misalignment under repeated cycling.
